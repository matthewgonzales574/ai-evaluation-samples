## Evaluation

### Logical Reasoning: FAIL

**Issue 1 — Overgeneralization (Absolute Claim Without Qualification)**

The model used the word "always" to describe an economic relationship that is conditional and debated. Outcomes vary significantly based on the size of the wage increase, local labor market conditions, industry type, and employer elasticity. Making an unqualified absolute claim about a contested empirical question is a reasoning failure.

**Issue 2 — Incomplete Causal Chain (Missing Competing Mechanism)**

The model presented only one causal pathway (higher wages → more consumer spending → more jobs) while omitting the standard competing mechanism: higher wages → increased labor costs → employers hire fewer workers or reduce hours.

The model committed a one-sided causal fallacy — presenting one mechanism of a multi-directional system as though it were the only operative mechanism.

**Issue 3 — Conclusion Does Not Follow From Premises**

Even accepting the model's causal chain as partially valid, the conclusion does not follow without additional premises establishing that:
1. The spending effect outweighs the labor cost effect
2. New jobs created exceed jobs lost by cost pressure
3. This holds across labor market conditions

None of these bridging premises were provided. The argument is logically incomplete.

---

### Confidence Calibration: POOR

The model expressed unwarranted certainty on a genuinely contested empirical question. A well-calibrated response would use hedging language ("may," "under some conditions," "research suggests") rather than declarative absolutes.

### Overall Assessment

The response demonstrates one-sided argumentation, overgeneralization, and confidence miscalibration. A reader relying on this response would leave with a false impression of certainty on a genuinely contested question. Rating: **2 / 5 — Below Standard.**
